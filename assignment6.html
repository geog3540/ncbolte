Interpretation of flow patterns

If you load my project into FlowMapper and then increase “Top Flow” values from 50 flows to 100, or 500, you’ll see things become more convoluted and quickly understand why California has such a high median inward flow, yet on the 50 limited map I made, it doesn’t show up. You see, I believe this is because it’s a juggernaut of a state both in size and economic opportunity. When increasing the Top Flows count to 500 or more, you start to see that flows come from just about every state in the lower 48, which would make mapping the migration to California nearly impossible in terms of its perceptual imagery being easy for anyone to read. The way I have it set up, in my opinion, helps highlight the most interesting changes between states in these 3+ decades. By far, Oklahoma attracts not only the most flows, but the largest classes of flows in this map. While I am not a knowledgeable history buff, it’d be interesting to read up on why this net gain occurred in Oklahoma of all places. Could it have been due to their oil or farming industry? Overall, it seems like the choropleth aspect of this map (especially places that didn’t qualify as part of the top 50 flows) shows a trend of westward movement in the United States, with most polygons in the Great Plains and west toward the Rocky Mountains and Pacific Ocean seeing net gains, and the flows that do exist on the map also seem to suggest that (with the big outlier being Oklahoma, once again). Perhaps by this point, enough farmland had been homesteaded to the point that people looking for more prosperity were driven further west, or even to Oklahoma. Despite Texas seeing net gains, the vast majority of flows going out from that neighboring state went straight to OK. Another interesting observation I made was how, when limited to the top 50 flows, there’s a clear trail of sorts made between Virginia and Iowa, showcasing that continual westward travel of people from that time period living in the United States.
